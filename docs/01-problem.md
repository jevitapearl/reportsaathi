Problem statement

Adults over 40, especially those on long-term medication, leave a consultation without understanding why each tablet matters or what its side effects mean. The doctor explains too fast and in jargon, and a second explanation is hard to get because it takes time, travel, or someone who is simply unavailable.

Two things in that statement are unproven: how often it causes harm (you gave me no estimate), and whether patients would use a tool over their pharmacist or a relative.

Option A: Low-software “medicine explainer” cards

Pharmacists and doctors write short plain-language explanations for the 30-50 most common chronic medicines, in the local language, covering what it’s for, how to take it, expected effects, and red flags. These go out as printed cards at pharmacies and clinics, and as pre-recorded WhatsApp voice notes sent when someone messages the medicine’s name.

Assumptions

A small set of common drugs covers most of the confusion.
Pharmacies or clinics will hand out cards or promote the WhatsApp number.
Patients prefer voice and print in their own language over typing.
Doctors or pharmacists will review the content without being paid much.

Medical risks: Content goes stale as guidance changes. A generic card can’t account for a patient’s other drugs or conditions. A patient may read “this side effect is common” and miss that theirs is serious.

Legal risks: Mostly liability for inaccurate content. You’d need clear disclaimers and a named clinical reviewer. Data risk is low if you only store phone numbers, but even that triggers data protection duties.
